## Defining Intelligence

I will begin with my search for a definitive way of determining if an agent is intelligent. Generally experts agree that intelligence involves three major factors: learning from experience, recognizing problems, and solving problems (Cherry 2022). Learning from experience is defined as “the acquisition, retention, and use of knowledge (...)” (Cherry 2022). For an agent to be intelligent it must be able to acquire new knowledge from its experiences, store the information for future experiences, and then apply this knowledge to a similar or new problem. Next is recognizing problems: “To use knowledge, (...) must identify the problems it might address.” (Cherry 2022). This is the aspect where an agent must be able to identify and find patterns in the world in which its existing knowledge can be applied. Lastly, the agent must be able to use what it has learned to come up with solutions to problems.

However, one can dig deeper in finding the meaning to intelligence. While these three steps are the defining features of an agent being intelligent, intelligence can then be further categorized. Psychologist Robert Sternberg defines a successful intelligent agent as involving three factors: Analytical Intelligence, Creative Intelligence, and Practical intelligence (Cherry 2022). Analytical intelligence is the ability to evaluate information and solve problems. Creative intelligence is the ability to come up with new ideas. Practical intelligence is the ability to adapt to a changing environment. The key features of this new definition is the concept of creating new ideas and adapting to the environment around it. I may now begin to argue that the creation of new ideas and the successful adaptation to the environment might require consciousness.

In the paper Universal Intelligence: A definition of machine intelligence they construct a measurement of intelligence: “Intelligence measures an agent’s ability to achieve goals in a wide range of environments” (Legg and Hunter, 2007). This definition draws a parallel to Cherry’s definition. In order for a machine to have a higher level of intelligence, it must be able to achieve its goal in a number of environments. Thus it must be able to adapt to a number of environments, which requires the development of new ways to solve its given problem; the creation of new ideas. Legg and Hunter further their definition by stating, “...in its lowest terms intelligence is present where the individual (...) is aware (...) of the relevance of his behavior to an objective” (Legg and Hunter 2007). This argues that there must be a level of awareness of the agent. It must be aware of itself and its environment in order to choose its actions to achieve its objective. However, the existence of a goal raises the problem of how the agent knows what its goal is; the answer may be consciousness.

## What is Consciousness?

Consciousness is considered a biological phenomenon that scientists and researchers have still yet to fully define. However, it can be described in the way of its subjectivity; “There is a sense in which each person’s consciousness is private to that person, a sense in which he is related to his pains, tickles, itches, thoughts and feelings in a way that is quite unlike the way others are related to those (...) feelings.” (Searle 2002). In the previous milestone, I considered diving deeper into Searle’s belief that intentionality is a core feature of consciousness. He defines intentionality as the “feature of certain mental states and events that consists in their being directed at, being about, being of, or representing certain other entities and states of affairs” (Searle 2022). However, after diving more into his paper, I believe that this concept is irrelevant in determining if an agent is capable of achieving consciousness.

Subjective Experience however is more in the direction I would like to take my research. Searle talked about how each person’s consciousness is “private” to that person, in that they are experiencing the world in a uniquely subjective way. One of the challenges of implementing consciousness into an artificially intelligent agent is described by a neuroscientist, “All digital computers (...) store and process information exclusively in terms of two states, which are represented by different symbols - in this case 1s and 0s” (Azarian, 2020). The neuroscientist believed that consciousness is not possible by machines given our simple binary system that computers are built upon. However, this answer only considers the technology available to us today. The article states, “There is nothing about processing symbols or computation that generates subjective experience or psychological phenomena like qualitative sessions” (Azarian 2020). With this taken into account, maybe the missing step in technology that would allow programs to gain consciousness is subjective experience. The author of the journal continues on about subjective consciousness, “In philosophy and psychology literature, emotion is considered a biological reaction and subjective experience that may not be easily disentangled into binary computing elements. (...) emotion is no different from cognition and can be similarly programmed, given sufficient programming skill (...)” (Azarian 2020). He argues another missing element from artificial intelligence that limits its ability to be intelligent is its inability to process emotions; Emotion is necessary for subjective experience. Thus, one of the elements of AI that limits its ability to be “intelligent” is subjective consciousness.

## Subjective Experience and Scientific Perspectives

In continuation of defining consciousness, subjective experience plays a large role within the scientific community around consciousness. Michael Graziano discusses subjective experience in his book “Rethinking Consciousness: A Scientific Theory of Subjective Experience ”. He states, “Consciousness is a hard problem because it’s a private experience; it cannot be confirmed from the outside” (Graziano 2019). He then continues, “By the very nature of subjective experience, you can’t push on it and measure a reaction force, put it on a scale and measure how much it weighs, or heat it and measure its combustion temperature (...) It’s immune to science.” His argument claims that science will never be able to understand consciousness because it is yet to be defined or measured; It is an unmeasurable intangible concept. Without a concrete definition, there is not much that can be simulated. Behaviors of a conscious entity can be simulated. However, the thoughts and processes behind consciousness are an anomaly that cannot be explained or measured. Graziano continues, “The confusion arises when people ask, ‘I understand that you can measure information, but how will you know if the machine also has that inner feeling? Only the machine can directly experience its own consciousness, so who besides the machine can ever really know?’” (Graziano 2019). This is one of the largest obstacles in developing an artificially conscious agent. How does one measure its conscious ability? How does one know if the machine is conscious? The concept of subjective experience behind consciousness points to the fact that only the machine itself would be able to know if it is conscious, not the creators of it.

Another relevant piece of literature is “Human Brain and Artificial Intelligence” by J. Weng; It considers the question “Can machines be conscious?” and presents Autonomous Programming for General Purposes (APFGP) in a practical characterization of consciousness; It argues that conscious machines - specifically APFGP’s - can become maturely conscious through lifelong development without considering all of the necessary criteria of human consciousness: vision, audition, language processing, etc. The book also aims to answer the two questions “How can human brain research inspire the study of AI?” and “How can AI and brain intelligence be combined to build a more powerful one?”. Weng concluded that there are eight requirements for an agent to obtain consciousness: Grounded, Emergent, Natural, Incremental, Skulled, Attentive, Motivated, and Abstractive; this was given the acronym GENISAMA. Grounded means the agent must have sensors and effectors directly grounded to the physical world. Emergent is another key factor; Weng states, “An artificial genetic algorithm without lifetime learning/development does not have anything to emerge since (...) (it) does not learn/develop in life” (Weng 2021). Therefore, an agent must be able to develop and learn from its experiences. Natural is in terms of having natural sensory and motor signals instead of human involved features from sensors and human made “labels”. The agent must be able to perceive the world from its own point of view. Incremental is the aspect of allowing past experience to affect current experience; learning must take place incrementally in time. Skulled refers to the idea that once the agent's “brain” is set free into the world, there is no tampering with its function. There are no adjustments to how it operates or sees the world. Attentive is more about the agent's awareness of itself to the situation it is in. It focuses on whether the agent is asking questions such as “what am I doing?” or “am I doing the right thing?”. This is almost the “awareness” aspect of consciousness. Next is Motivated. Motivation is not necessarily as obvious as it seems. The agent must “(...) learn motivation based on its intrinsic motives, such as pain avoidance, pleasure seeking, uncertainty awareness, and sensitivity to novelty. A system that is designed to do facial recognition does not have motive to do things other than facial recognition.” (Weng 2021). There is almost a level of autonomy for an agent to be motivated; it must be able to choose its own path and desires so that it seeks more than the task in front of it. Lastly, an agent must be Abstractive to obtain consciousness. Weng states, “Although a shallow definition of consciousness means awareness, full awareness requires a general capability to abstract higher concepts from concrete examples” (Weng 2021). The agent must be able to think at a higher level than the input sensory that it is receiving. It must be able to see the big picture.

## Artificial Consciousness and Emotion

In my initial research of consciousness and artificial intelligence I quickly learned of the field of artificial consciousness. Artificial consciousness is a field that works relatively alongside artificial intelligence with separate goals and criteria for success. In the journal Artificial Conscious Intelligence (Reggia, Katz, Davis 2020), the authors present ideas on how to integrate artificial consciousness into artificial intelligence systems, which could produce smarter, more effective artificial intelligence systems. One of the challenges of implementing consciousness into an artificially intelligent entity is described by a neuroscientist, “All digital computers (...) store and process information exclusively in terms of two states, which are represented by different symbols - in this case 1s and 0s.” (Azarian, 2020). The neuroscientist believed that consciousness is not possible by machines given our simple binary system that all computers built upon. However, this answer only considers the technology available to us today. The article states, “There is nothing about processing symbols or computation that generates subjective experience or psychological phenomena like qualitative sensations” (Azarian 2020). Azarian continues using Turing machines as an example, “A Turing machine’s operations are said to be ‘syntactical’, meaning they only recognize symbols and not the meaning of those symbols”. This further proves that some kind of awareness is necessary for an agent to be intelligent; consciousness is key to intelligence. It is irrelevant how fast a computer is, what kind of problems it can solve, etc. if it cannot comprehend where it stands in this world. This was proven by John Searle in his Chinese Room argument: “(...) a strict symbol-processing machine can never be a symbol-understanding machine” (Azarian 2020). There needs to be a higher level of thinking for the machine.

Michael Graziano considered the practicality of building a conscious machine in his book “Rethinking Consciousness: A Scientific Theory of Subjective Experience”. He claims that there are four components necessary for creating a conscious machine: artificial attention, attention schema, content, and a sophisticated search engine. The first component, artificial attention, requires the machine to “ (...) have the ability to focus its processing resources and to shift that focus from object to object - from a nearby able, to a doughnut, to a person standing at the far side of the room, to an entirely internal event such as a recalled memory.” (Graziano 2019). Attention he argues is not the hardest component to implement into the machine; Machines today have a form of simulated attention noted as systematic management of resources. Networks and computers today are capable of systematically managing the resources it is given. However, the difficulty comes in the abstract thinking that must accompany systematic management: “Human attention has a specific suite of properties. When it is focused on an item, it enables you to process the item in detail and extract deeper meaning from it. Attention enables you to identify the items affordances (...) can it be grasped, kicked, bitten?” (Graziano 2019). This is a form of parallel processing that goes alongside the machine’s attention. It provides extra information on top of what is being processed by the machine. Most of all, the machine must be able to shift its attention from different domains of information. Graziano uses today’s facial recognition machines as an example of the technology’s attention limitations: “A simple version of spatial attention can point the camera at the face you want to identify, then the recognition algorithm goes to work. The machine never needs to shift its focus of attention across multiple domains of information, from faces to sounds to internal thoughts, because it works only within the one domain of facial recognition” (Graziano 2019). The second component, attention schema, is an internal model that informs the machine about subjective consciousness. In other words, it is a schema for the machine to monitor and manage its own attention. Graziano argues that the attention schema is one of the easiest and most circumscribed components to build into the machine. The third component, content, is a broad set of information for the machine to operate on. Emotions are part of this component. Emotion can be seen as attached to this component because emotions are processed parallel to information. One can feel a certain way about information that is being processed visually, audibly, physically, and so on. It is a prospect of consciousness that even the conscious being may be unaware of its existence when processing information: “To give an everyday example, you might feel agitated, stressed, or angry at a low level and have no explicit idea of it. Then a friend points out, ‘Wow, what kind of mood are you in?; and you redirect your attention - and awareness - and realize, ‘Huh, I really am stressed’.” (Graziano 2019). Thus, while picking the base set of information the machine starts with is a factor, another aspect to it must be how the machine itself feels about the information. For example, “When you look at your least favorite politician and feel angry, that’s your amygdala making the association between vision and emotion.” (Graziano 2019). Very similar to the first component, attention, content must also have a parallel processing unit that does not take a set of information at face value; it must be processed alongside emotions to create abstract thoughts and feelings. The fourth and final component needed is a talking search engine. The machine must be able to understand verbal, visual, and audible information and search a database for its own knowledge about the matter regardless of the form: “(...) the human brain can translate speech into nonverbal information and back again. If someone asks you, ‘How does the taste of lemon compare with that of an orange?’ you don’t answer the same way an internet search engine does. You don’t rely on word association. You translate the speech into taste information and compare the two remembered tastes, then translate the answer back into words (...)” (Graziano 2019). This then creates the problem of representing different types of information. How does a computer store taste information? How does a computer store emotions behind different forms of information? Words and numbers can only provide the computer a limited representation of different concepts.

Emotions play a key role in natural human interaction. Marvin Minsky stated, “The question is not whether intelligent machines can have any emotions, but whether machines can be intelligent without any emotions.” (Bartneck, Lyons, Saerbeck 2017). This question is quite interesting; it infers that emotions are of greater importance to intelligence than previously understood. The OCC model is a standard model developed for emotion synthesis. It was built upon previous models that were designed to synthesize emotions. While the model is complex, the process of developing emotion from the model can be broken down into four distinct phases: Categorization, Quantification, Interaction, and Mapping. These four distinct phases must be present in an artificially intelligent agent for processing emotions. In the Categorization phase, “an event, action, or object is evaluated by the character which results in information on what emotional categories are affected. This categorization requires the character to know the relation of a particular object, for example, to its attitudes.” (Bartneck, Lyons, Saerbeck 2017). This is where love and hate comes in. The agent must have enough awareness of itself and the world to comprehend its own emotional stance on a particular issue or event. One way to store this knowledge would be concatenating a massive table of all the possible actions, events, and objects mapped to emotional categories and their respective intensities. Quantification is “the intensity of an emotional category (...) defined separately for events, actions, and objects. The intensity of the emotional categories resulting from an event is defined as the desirability and for actions and objects praiseworthiness and appealingness respectively.” (Bartneck, Lyons, Saerbeck 2017). In short, Quantification is the intensity of the emotional category that the agent feels towards different things such as actions, events, and objects. The authors emphasize the importance of the agent's personal goals in determining quantification. The key word they use is desirability in explaining its importance in relation to their banana example: “The first time the character receives a banana, it will use its default likelihood to calculate the desirability of the event. When the character receives the next banana, it will look at the history and calculate how often it received a banana in the last moments. The more often it receives a banana in the past the higher is the likelihood of this event and hence the lower is its desirability.” (Bartneck, Lyons, Saerbeck 2017). Mapping occurs when the emotion model has more categories than the agent has abilities to express. It must then map the emotional category to an available form of expression. Interaction is simply the interaction between classification and quantification to determine its emotional state. These four steps would be necessary for an agent to process emotions. However, the question of whether an agent needs emotion to be intelligent was answered, “A problem that all these artificial entities have to deal with is, that while their expression processing has reached an almost sufficient maturity, their intelligence has not. This is especially problematic, since the mere presence of an animated face raises the expectation levels of its user.” (Bartneck, Lyons, Saerbeck 2017). The author continues, “The same holds true for speech. If an artificial entity talks then we also expect it to listen and understand. (...) Emotion models heavily rely on the progress made in artificial intelligence and hence I would like to reply to Minsky’s statement with a question: ‘Will emotional machines have intelligence’” (Bartneck, Lyons, Saerbeck 2017). In a form, the author is arguing that emotional machines and artificially intelligent machines are two separate entities. However, both are needed if we want to achieve true intelligence in a human-made agent.

## Simulation vs. Duplication

Given these requirements for consciousness, it is easy to forget that simulation does not equal duplication. Scientists are currently deconstructing how physical, biochemical, and electrical processes create the idea of “consciousness”. While we may have been able to simulate different brain functions that mimic intelligence, it is clearly not enough to make the agent a conscious problem solver. Azarian states, “Much like a computer, neurons communicate with one another through exchanging electrical signals in a binary faction”, he continues, “But unlike digital computers, brains contain a host of analogue cellular and molecular processes, biochemical reactions, electrostatic forces, global synchronized neuron firing at specific frequencies, and unique structural and functional connects with countless feedback loops” (Azarian 2020). Without a full understanding of how these different processes affect brain function, we are limited in our ability to duplicate higher-level thinking. We can merely simulate the resulting behaviors and actions that are an outcome of these different processes. Without proper understanding of the processes that create these resulting behaviors, we are merely simulating the behavior, not the thoughts.

## Ethical Implications

One of the biggest questions arising when discussing consciousness in machines is the ethical implications of doing so. A fictional film that elaborates on this topic is “Solo: A Star Wars Story”. Dan Hassler-Forest in a Washington Post article states, “(...) Anakin Skywalker, himself a child slave, build C-3PO, again without a second thought about confining his creation to its own (eternal) life of servitude. It all seems so obvious in retrospect, and yet the master-servant relationship between organic and artificial life in the Star Wars franchise has been largely ignored (...)”. He then continues, “We know that droids in the Star Wars universe can be bought, sold, disassembled and tortured without consequence, and (...) while we perceive them in roughly the same way as (...) characters, with personalities, emotions, quirks, flaws, genders, and even sexual identities and desires, (...) their consciousness can’t be reduced to preprogrammed personalities.” (Hassler-Forect 2018). The ethical implications of designing conscious sentient machines with the purpose of a living a life of servitude are enormous; it is a form of slavery. While yes, they are machines, the mere fact that personalities, emotions, and desires are aspects of their consciousness does beg the question of whether it is ethical to create such machines. Steve Torrance in his article “The Ethical Status of Artificial Agents” argues, “(...) think for a moment of some favorite hi-tech possession: laptop, cellphone, car, iPod, or whatever. Now imagine that this device is actually conscious - its electronic circuits and computations have really produced an inner awareness so that it feels, desires, has satisfactions and sufferings. (...) how different would your attitude be towards a device if it (...) were a genuinely conscious machine? Would you still be able to adopt a merely instrumental attitude towards it, or would you be tempted to be concerned about how the world was consciously experienced from its point of view?” (Torrance 2006). The concern of the author is genuine. With the adoption of such conscious machines we enter a new space where the rights of such machines would have to be considered. No conscious being should be subjected to an eternal life of servitude, while still having the ability to question its position and feelings towards its own reality.

## Can AI Be Intelligent Without Consciousness?

Finally, I need to provide evidence on the other side of my research question; That is can an artificially intelligent entity be intelligent without consciousness. This can be explored by looking at the limits of artificial intelligence. Today’s artificial intelligence’s primary focus is on dealing with large magnitudes of data; this is a task it is good at. This can be broken down into two different forms of intelligence: strong intelligence and weak intelligence. Weak intelligence can be seen as “AI specializes in one task; machines can only perform the task for which they were programmed. (...) Within weak intelligence, we can consider applications such as image or character recognition, voice recognition or autonomous driving systems” (Sandra 2020). Most of the “intelligent” agents we have today fall into the category of weak intelligence. That is solving a given task in a highly efficient manner. Strong intelligence refers to “machines that can perform intellectual activities just like human beings” (Sandra 2020). This includes reasoning, planning, solving problems, and thinking abstractly. There are not any examples of strongly intelligent agents in today's world; a clear-cut example of strong intelligence is C3PO from the movie Star Wars. Until we can establish the concepts of Abstraction and Motivated, strong intelligence will be a thought of the future. Next, Bin Liu asks and answers the question “What is the greatest value of ‘Weak AI’ for us?” in an academic journal. The author also answers the questions “Will ‘Weak AI’ grow into ‘Strong AI’?”. Liu states, “In a foreseeable future, ‘weak AI’ cannot develop into ‘strong AI’, but it provides a channel to synthesize advances obtained from related disciplines (...).” (Liu 2021). Liu is arguing that weak AI will always be “weak”. In the example of using a Turing test to determine if a machine is intelligent, he argues that this test is a clear example of this; Machines will only develop to be able to pass the test. This kind of specialty intelligence is not true intelligence, it is just efficiency.

After my research, I am now able to answer the following questions: “What is consciousness?”, “Can consciousness be mimicked by a computer?”, and “How can consciousness improve artificial intelligence?”. I have been able to conclude that there are several factors that play into consciousness than the computational aspect of it; There are biochemical, electrical, emotional, and physical processes that have an effect on conscious thought. An intelligent agent must have a form of higher-level thinking that allows abstract thought and problem solving in scenarios that it has not been introduced to. Thus, consciousness is the key to self-awareness and subjective experience. Both are required for the agent to understand its place in the world and operate in new scenarios based on past experiences. I now believe that consciousness is an absolute requirement for human-perceived intelligence. Moreover, the agent must also have some form of emotional understanding of the events and objects that happen around it. It must have its own opinions and attitudes that can affect its decision making so that the agent can reach its desired goals. Finally, after my research I can argue that I do not believe we are capable of programming consciousness into a machine. As stated earlier, there are biochemical, electrical, emotional, and physical processes that all have an affect on conscious being and thought. Without a full replication and understanding of these processes combined with the limited binary system computers are built upon I do not believe that synthesized consciousness is possible in a machine. However, the new question arises of “should we attempt to create conscious entities?”. My answer is no. The ethical implications of creating an agent that has the ability to comprehend its “life” and situation should not be subjected to a life of slavery to humans. If we give an entity consciousness, then we are giving the agents the same thought processes as humans. Thus, lifelong servitude of a conscious being is inhumane.
